Where the channels meet

Every VLA must join vision, language and telemetry somewhere before it can emit an action. The component lists have converged; the join topology has not. This is the taxonomy that survives that convergence — and the one that says where a failure can live and where a probe can go.

Part I · The framing

Why the component list stopped discriminating

For essentially every policy released since 2025, the recipe reads the same: a SigLIP-family vision encoder, a pretrained LLM backbone, and a chunked continuous action decoder trained by flow matching or diffusion. Even OpenVLA-OFT abandoned discrete action tokens for parallel continuous decoding. At the resolution of "which ViT, which LLM, which head", most of the field is one architecture.

That is precisely why it is the wrong resolution to work at. If the components no longer vary, component identity cannot predict whether a failure found on one policy exists on another — and transfer of findings is the whole question.

What still varies is structural. Three input channels — pixels, text, proprioception — have to be joined before an action comes out, and there are only a few ways to join them. The choice determines which internal states exist at all, and therefore which failures are even reachable. Two policies can share every component and differ here completely.

The concrete case this document was written around

π0 and GR00T N1.7 carry the same family label — VLM plus continuous action expert — and have opposite topologies. In N1.7 the robot state bypasses the vision-language backbone entirely and meets the VL stream only inside the DiT, so no single embedding space holds all three channels upstream of System 1. In π0 the action expert attends within the same stream as the VLM, and state is a token in that stream, so such a space exists.

Same cluster. Different mechanism. Different answer to "where did this failure come from."

A second hazard follows from the same place: published architecture figures routinely flatten the join. The GR00T N1 paper figure draws a single merge point where the code has three distinct mechanisms — cross-attention for VL, concatenation for state and noised actions, and self-attention within the backbone. A diagram that cannot tell those apart cannot be used for mechanism reasoning. The diagrams below therefore draw edges by join kind, not as undifferentiated arrows.

Part V · Training

What actually gets trained

The short answer to the reuse question: no, you cannot drop a pretrained VLM into a robot and get actions out of a forward pass. Something always has to be trained on robot data. But far less than people assume, and the part that is reused is the expensive part.

Three reasons the free-forward-pass version cannot work. Actions are not in a VLM’s output distribution — it emits text, while a robot needs a continuous vector in an embodiment-specific space with its own units, frame conventions and gripper semantics, none of which web pretraining constrains. The action head is randomly initialised in every topology above, so there is no path that avoids fitting it. And the one attempt to dodge this by casting actions as text tokens still required co-fine-tuning on robot data: reusing the vocabulary is not the same as reusing the behaviour.

What is genuinely free is the perceptual and semantic representation. That is the real value of the VLM, and it is why these models work at all given how little robot data exists: the robot data only has to learn a mapping from an already-good representation to actions, rather than learn to see.

Component by component

The part that blocks true cross-embodiment reuse

State and action encoders are per-embodiment and have no pretrained counterpart — there is no web corpus of joint angles. So even a model marketed as cross-embodiment grows new parameters for a new robot, and those parameters see only that robot’s data. Whatever generalisation exists lives in the shared middle, not at the edges.

The stages, and why the distinction matters for reading numbers

A comparison trap

A reported “zero-shot” number usually means stage 2 only; a LIBERO number usually means stage 3 on LIBERO demonstrations. These are different models with the same name. Comparing across that line — or reproducing one while reading the other’s published figure — is the most common way a reproduction silently fails.

The shared data substrate

The overlap here is larger than the architectural overlap. Most of the roster pretrains on some mixture of the same aggregated corpus, which is worth knowing when asking whether two policies would fail on the same inputs.

Why “both trained on OXE” is a weak claim

Open X-Embodiment is an aggregation, and every paper reweights it — dropping subsets, upweighting Bridge or Fractal, filtering by camera or by success. Two models both described as OXE-pretrained can share little effective data. The mixture, not the corpus name, is the thing that would predict shared failure, and it is usually reported loosely or not at all.

Objectives

The loss follows the action decoder, not the join topology — which is why it is a weaker taxonomy than the one above, and why two structurally opposite policies can share an objective.

The one thing every objective on this page has in common

All of it is behaviour cloning. No policy on the roster trains against its own failures in the base recipe — there is no reward, no negatives, no contrastive term over things that went wrong. Failure is therefore out of distribution with respect to the objective, not merely the data.

That is the structural reason failure mining has something to find: these models have never been given a representation of what going wrong looks like, so nothing in training pressures them to be calibrated about it.

Standing rule for this document

The same rule covers Part V: the dataset scales there are order-of-magnitude recall, not counts read from a dataset card, and the stage boundaries are a generalisation that individual recipes violate. Neither is safe to quote.

An amber exemplar may be cited for its shape but not for a number. Before any amber claim is used to justify an experimental design, it gets promoted to green by reading the implementation — the same bar the rest of the project uses.
