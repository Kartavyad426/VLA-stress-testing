From one robot failure to the data that fixes it

A worked example of the failure-scenario mining spec, built from one real failure in our simulator. Draft for stakeholder review · 2026-10-08.

A robot policy fails at a task. Generic "more training data" rarely fixes it. What does fix it is examples of the exact moment where it went wrong, done right. The mining spec is how we write that moment down, so that we can search existing data for it, brief a person to demonstrate it, or recreate it in simulation.

This page follows one real failure through the first two steps: turning it into a spec entry, and searching for successful examples of it. Labels: MEASURED means taken from our run files. ILLUSTRATIVE means a setting we chose for this example. MOCK-UP means not built yet.

1The failure

The robot is a standard research arm, controlled by GR00T N1.7, NVIDIA's open robot model, in the version NVIDIA tuned for this set of tabletop tasks. The instruction is: "pick up the black bowl between the plate and the ramekin and place it on the plate".

Normally every episode starts with the arm in the same pose. Here we started it slightly off that pose: the hand began 9.6 cm from its usual spot. MEASURED Real robots rarely start in exactly the same place, so this is a realistic stress. In our test of this scene from this offset, 5 of 6 runs succeeded. Across all ten scenes, success fell from 57/60 at the normal start to 44/60 at this offset and 23/60 at a larger one. MEASURED

Here is the failed run. The top row of each frame is the scene camera, the bottom row is the camera on the robot's wrist. These are the two images the policy sees.

It then keeps trying. On the sixth try it lifts the bowl 10 cm, drops it again, and runs out of time after 8 attempts and 14 s. MEASURED

2Find where it went wrong: the root

A failure like this is a chain: miss, retry, retry, lift, drop, time out. Fixing the end of the chain (better retries) is not the point. The root is the first thing that went wrong, and everything after it is a consequence.

We compare against a second run that started from exactly the same pose and succeeded. The only difference is the random variation inside the policy. The two runs leave the start together. At 1.2 s their hands are 3 cm apart, and at 1.55 s the failing run closes its grip in the wrong place. MEASURED

Run 1 · failedRun 2 · same start, succeededHeight of the bowl above the table, cm

So the root is the grasp, and what went wrong is a slip: the fingers caught only the edge of the rim, the bowl rose 2 cm and fell out. Both runs started in the same place, so the start pose alone did not decide the outcome. What decided it is where the hand was when it closed. That moment, not the whole episode, is what we need successful examples of.

3Write it down: the spec entry

The root becomes one entry with four parts. Each part answers one question, and each value is read from the simulator, not typed by hand. MEASURED

Root

Where did it first go wrong?

The grasp · failure mode slip
at 1.55 s, the first time the fingers close

Situation

What did the robot see and where was it, at that moment?

Hand 6.8 cm short of the bowl's centre, 3.6 cm to the side, 1.3 cm above it; gripper 4° from vertical; plate, ramekin and the other objects where they were
plus both camera images at that moment

Success

What does "got past it" mean?

The bowl is lifted at least 5 cm within 2 seconds of the fingers closing ILLUSTRATIVE

Outcome

How did the episode end?

Ran out of time after 8 grasp attempts; bowl lifted once and dropped; ended 6 cm from the plate

One more thing the entry records is the cause. Here that's the off-pose start, but it's kept as a condition, not as part of what the failure is. A grasp slip is the same physical failure whether it was caused by an odd start, a moved camera or a bumped table. Keeping causes separate means the same kind of failure can be counted and fixed across tasks, robots and customers.

4Search for successes

We then run the entry as a query: find moments where a robot or a person was in this same situation, and the grasp worked. We searched two pools for this task:

  • Human demonstrations: the 50 demonstrations people recorded for this exact task, which is the data this policy was tuned on. They contain 53 successful grasps.
  • Our own successful runs of this scene: 56 episodes from our robustness tests, with 56 successful grasps.

A grasp counts as a match only if, at the moment the fingers start closing, the hand is within 1.5 cm of the failure's position relative to the bowl in every direction, the gripper is angled within 20° of it, and the objects on the table are laid out within 3 cm of the same arrangement. What came back

From the human demonstrations: nothing. The people who recorded them always closed the gripper nearer the bowl's centre. The furthest-back successful human grasp started 4.6 cm short of centre; the failure was 6.8 cm short. Widening the window to ±4 cm still finds no human demo that also has the same table layout, because the demonstrations were recorded with the objects placed slightly differently. MEASURED

This empty result is the finding. The data the policy learned from never shows it how to finish a grasp from this position. When the hand ends up there, the policy has nothing to imitate.

From our own runs: one match. In another test run the arm started from a different off-pose start (0.4 instead of 0.2 in our units) but reached almost the same spot at the moment of grasping, and that time the grasp held. That's exactly what the search is designed to find: the same situation, reached by a different path. It's also a borderline match, within the window by a few millimetres on each axis, so a stricter window of ±1 cm returns nothing. MEASURED

The search returns this segment, from the fingers closing to the lift, ready to use as a training example for this exact situation.

5Filling the gap MOCK-UP

Not built yet. This section shows what would happen next. Nothing below has been run.

The search found one example where we would want many. Because the failure happened in simulation, we can recreate the exact moment it went wrong and produce more successes from it:

  • Recreate the moment: put the arm, bowl and objects back exactly as they were at 1.55 s.
  • Finish it correctly: let a scripted expert or a human operator complete the grasp from there, varying the start of the moment slightly each time, so the examples cover the area around it rather than a single point.
  • Check each one against the entry's own success test: the bowl is lifted 5 cm within 2 seconds.

The same entry would also brief a person to set up the situation on a real robot, or tell a sim pipeline what to generate. Those are the entry's two other uses. They are not shown here.

6What a full spec gives you

This page followed one entry. A spec covers a policy in one deployment setting, with an entry for each distinct failure found. Each entry is readable by a person and searchable by a machine. For each one, it says:

  • where the failure starts, and what the robot saw and where it was at that moment;
  • what success at that moment looks like, as a test any new example can be checked against;
  • whether existing data already covers it (search results attached) or it's a gap that needs new data, and what kind.

Failure types use shared names (here, grasp · slip). That means counts can be compared across policies and datasets without exposing one customer's details to another.
