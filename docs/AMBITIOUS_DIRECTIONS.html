Ambitious directions for the VLA project

Discussion draft, 2026-09-29. Each direction builds on something we have already shown. See docs/VLA_doc_pending.html for where the base doc stands. Literature added 2026-09-29: about 35 papers found by web search. Most were checked against their arXiv abstract page only, not read in full; rows marked "I haven't read it" were only seen in search results. Classic references are from memory. Check before citing.

What we have that others don't

  • Mechanism evidence. Every perturbation reaches the action through the image tokens. For start pose it's the wrist camera, and correcting only the first action chunk rescues it. Camera shifts need correcting throughout the episode (R-039, R-042, R-057).
  • A way to generate corrected demos in simulation (R-054), and LoRA fine-tuning of GR00T that fits the 8 GB card.
  • Discipline: pre-registration, reproduction gates, no-op controls. Most VLA robustness papers don't have these.

Directions

1. The data-efficiency law for repairing a policy top pick

R-040 generalised. Take one failure region and compare equal data budgets of four kinds: demos targeted by mechanism, demos targeted by failure, random extra demos, and nominal demos. Plot a data-response curve for each.

  • Target headline: "targeted demos close X% of the gap at 1/10th the data."
  • That's iMerit's whole pitch as a measured number, and a publishable result.
  • It's the base doc's thesis done properly, and the R-053 to R-058 pipeline already runs it.

Risk: it needs many training runs. On 8 GB each LoRA run takes hours, so this probably wants a rented GPU.

2. Fixing the policy while it runs, with no retraining

R-057 says start-pose failures are decided in the first chunk. Correcting that chunk rescues 24/30, and the first action's size predicts failure (r = −0.36, R-047).

  • Build a monitor that flags "this start pose is off-distribution" from the first forward pass.
  • Then apply a cheap correction: re-home the arm, blend the first chunk, or steer the wrist-camera features.
  • Test-time repair versus retraining is an interesting comparison in its own right, and it's something customers could actually deploy.

Risk: R-037 found that nothing predicts failure well yet, so the monitor is the hard part.

Detection is crowded: at least eight papers in 2026. Open niche: fixing a bad start, where the episode is decided in the first chunk (R-057 k* = 1), rather than drift in the middle. Plus a cost comparison: repairing at run time against retraining on the same failure. Don't build another detector; use chunk agreement (VLA-FAIL) or SAFE as the trigger.

3. Steering the policy's internal features (mechanistic interpretability)

We know where perturbations live: the backbone output, the image tokens, the wrist camera.

  • Try activation patching or steering vectors that remove the start-pose or camera shift inside the network, so the policy corrects itself with no data at all.
  • If it works, it's a strong paper. If it doesn't, it bounds how far the "localised pathway" story goes.
  • It overlaps with the blocked action-atlas reproduction (arXiv:2603.19233), and we'd do it properly.

4. Do the mechanisms hold across architectures?

Rent a 24–80 GB GPU for about $50–200 and repeat the pathway study (R-039, R-042) on OpenVLA-OFT and π0.5.

  • If "vision tokens carry everything and the state input is inert" holds across three architectures, it's a general finding. If not, it's a comparison of architectures.
  • This also closes the base doc's OpenVLA-OFT gap.

5. Price human-made demos against simulated corrections

iMerit's actual business is human data.

  • Have annotators produce corrective demos in the simulator by teleoperation, or label failures, and compare their value per hour with our simulated corrections.
  • It answers the question the business needs answered: where are humans worth paying for?
  • Filling the empty human-review sheets (0/80 and 0/24) comes along for free.

6. Search for failures instead of sweeping grids

Replace fixed grids with Bayesian optimisation or active search over perturbation space. Find each boundary with 5–10x fewer rollouts, then scale to Isaac Lab-Arena. It's mostly engineering and less novel, but it's what a product would need.

7. Sim-to-real on a cheap arm

A LeRobot SO-101 costs about $250. Check whether the start-pose and camera boundaries found in simulation show up on a real arm.

Risk: it's the most convincing demo possible, but also the most hardware risk and the least reproducible.

Recommendation

Updated after the literature review: #1 is still the spine, but the cheapest fix is now an arm inside it. The central experiment: on one measured failure boundary (start pose, R-047), compare equal budgets of five repairs.

  1. Demos targeted by mechanism (the wrist-camera path, R-042).
  2. Demos targeted by failure (the R-054 rescues).
  3. Random extra demos.
  4. A parameter-only fix: visual-token rescaling or visual LoRA, the 2512.02902 approach.
  5. A run-time fix at the first chunk (from #2), with no training at all.

The headline is one figure: gap closed against cost (demos, GPU-hours, parameters). As far as I can find, nobody has published this comparison. #3, steering guided by mechanism, is the high-upside side project on the same failure.

Two checks the literature forces before R-056 is read: (i) whether the style of the minted rescue demos matches the pretraining demos (DATAFARM: planner-style demos barely transfer); (ii) whether the camera token-rescaling result reproduces on GR00T. If it does, the camera row may not need data at all.

For any of this to count, R-055 → R-056 has to pass first. #4 is the cheapest way to make everything more general, if there's any budget.

Cross-policy generality: evaluating a model we can't see inside

When a client hands us their model, they won't give us its architecture. Most of the harness survives a black box. The part that doesn't is the internal-pathway work, and most of it can be replaced by perturbing the inputs instead of the internals. Design around levels of access, and know which of our techniques each level supports.

What in our harness depends on GR00T internals (checked in the code)

Levels of access a client might give us

Level 0: API only. We send observations and get action chunks back.

  • Everything in the "No" rows above works.
  • Pathway attribution moves to the inputs. For example, perturb only the agent-view image and keep the wrist image nominal, or the reverse. That approximates R-042's which-camera answer without touching tokens. It isn't identical: a token splice isolates one internal channel exactly, while swapping input images also changes how the model combines the two cameras. But it answers the question a client cares about: which sensor is the policy fragile through.
  • Language pathway: swap only the instruction and hold the images fixed. We already do this at the input level (R-038, R-049).
  • Uncertainty, if the policy is stochastic: call it several times on the same observation and measure how much the samples spread.

Level 1: weights without documentation, e.g. a .pt file, ONNX or TorchScript.

  • We can find the hooks automatically. Walk the module graph and hook every block. Feed matched pairs (nominal vs perturbed image, image vs instruction change) and rank layers by how much each input moves them. The action head is the last block before the output with an action-sized shape.
  • That's our R-037 method turned into a search. It finds "where the image shift dies" without anyone telling us the architecture. It works on ONNX too, because the graph is inspectable.

Level 2: weights plus training access. Adds retraining, the pilot we're running now.

Level 3: full source. What we have with GR00T today.

Detecting failures in real time is the right black-box tool, with limits

The detectors that only need actions work at level 0:

  • Chunk agreement: do consecutive action chunks disagree? This is VLA-FAIL's ACC and Rewind-IL's TIDE.
  • Consistency across repeated samples: Sentinel's STAC. Caveat: Sentinel's score adds up over the episode, and our failures don't end early (all run to the 281-step timeout, against about 113 for successes), so used as published it partly measures episode length. Compare at the same forward index instead (docs/SENTINEL_METHODOLOGY.html §11).
  • Progress stalls: from simulator state.

Detectors that read internal features (SAFE, Mahalanobis distance on the last layer) need level 1. The limit, as the literature review found: detection is not diagnosis. It tells you when a run is going wrong, not why. The "why" at level 0 has to come from input counterfactuals.

What we'd still need from a client: a minimal input/output contract

A true zero-knowledge black box won't even run nominal episodes correctly. Our own history shows it: the SmolVLA gap, the 180° LIBERO image flip, gripper conventions, fp32 vs bf16. The minimum spec:

  • Inputs: which cameras, resolution, orientation, state vector layout, instruction format.
  • Actions: action space, normalisation, chunk length, control rate, gripper convention.
  • Controls: can we seed and reset it, and is it deterministic?
  • Gate: their own reported nominal number. We reproduce it before anything else, the same bar as MINERVA and SmolVLA.

That reproduction step is our conformance gate turned into a product feature: "we check we're running your model correctly before we judge it."

How to prove the harness works across policies

  1. Use our white-box results as ground truth for the black-box methods. On GR00T we know the answers: wrist camera for start pose, image tokens for everything, first chunk decides start pose. Run only the level-0 tools on GR00T as if it were a black box and score them: does input-level attribution recover what the token splices found, and do action-only detectors flag the failures that R-047 recorded? No one else has the internal ground truth to do this calibration.
  2. Repeat on a second policy with a different architecture, MINERVA, which we already have in its own venv. Run level-1 automatic hook discovery on it without using its source, then check the result against its real architecture.
  3. Write a level-by-level spec in the harness: a BlackBoxPolicy wrapper that exposes only reset(seed) and act(obs) -> chunk, plus an optional probe() for automatic hooks. Every technique declares its minimum access level, and the manifest records the level it was produced at.

Step 1 is cheap, needs no new GPU campaign beyond short runs, and gives a headline: "our black-box diagnosis agrees with white-box ground truth X% of the time." It also slots straight into direction #4.
