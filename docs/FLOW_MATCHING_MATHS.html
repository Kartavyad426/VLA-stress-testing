Direct answers
  • What flow matching is. It trains a velocity field vθ(x, t) whose ODE carries Gaussian noise to the data. The trick is that you never need the true (intractable) field. You regress on a per-sample target, here a − ε along the straight line from ε to a, and the gradient equals that of the intractable loss (§1.2, eq. 9). For a VLA, x is an action chunk and everything is conditioned on the observation o.
  • GR00T N1.7 in our repo, exactly code. Time runs from noise at t = 0 to data at t = 1. The noised chunk is xt = (1 − t)ε + t a, the target is a − ε, and the loss is a masked MSE. Training draws t = 0.999(1 − u) with u ~ Beta(1.5, 1), which puts most draws near the noise end. Inference is 4 Euler steps, querying the net at t = 0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75. Noise is a bare, unseeded torch.randn (groot_n1_7.py:554–708). π0's openpi/LeRobot code uses the opposite time direction from its own paper (§0).
  • One identity explains much of D20 derived. With Euler, the sampled chunk equals the network's one-shot data prediction at the last grid point: A = x + 0.25·vθ(x, 0.75), evaluated at the sampler's own state (eq. 31). A loss averaged over random t can fall a lot while that one slice moves little.
  • Why a 65% loss drop need not move the sample 65%. There are five separable reasons (§3.1).
    1. The loss is squared: a 65% drop in MSE is a ~40% drop in RMS error (0.159 → 0.055 ⇒ √ratio 0.59).
    2. The loss was measured on training starts and the ~10% movement on held-out starts. That is a coverage question that no flow argument removes.
    3. The loss averages over t, but the sample depends on the t = 0.75 slice, evaluated off the training interpolant.
    4. Mode mixing. If replay and minted labels disagree near the same observation, the flow keeps both modes and ε picks one.
    5. The first-chunk metric is a median while the loss is a tail-sensitive mean.
    Today's fit test settles it in favour of reason 2. On their own training starts the adapters closed 30% (r16) and 27% (r4) of the first-chunk gap, as reason 1 predicts. On held-out directions they closed 1–15%. The loss-to-sample pipeline works; the gap is coverage across directions × offsets (§3.1).
  • Noise. Each chunk is a deterministic map Φo(ε) of the noise. Comparing two observations under different ε adds a pure sampler term to every difference (eq. 41). That is why R-039's splices fix ε per forward, and why single-draw STAC on R-041 was noise-dominated. Render jitter still flips outcomes at fixed ε (R-039: 23/30 agree), so a fixed seed does not make rollouts reproducible.
  • FACT's "training mismatch" paper. The Beta(1.5, 1) schedule puts only 8.9% of draws in the near-data fifth of time, where fine contact corrections are made. A logit-normal shifted toward the data end gives 6× more (we recompute both numbers in §3.4). GR00T uses the same schedule, so the 8.9% holds for it too. But GR00T's 4-step sampler never queries t > 0.75, and the velocity parametrisation already weights late-t errors by 1/(1 − t)². How much this matters for us is open inferred.
  • Practical implications for retraining. Select and report with sampled chunks at fixed, paired ε (K = 4 as deployed, several ε per start), not with loss. Log the loss per t-bin. Treat a schedule change (FACT's logit-normal) as a pre-registered arm, not a default. Our fine-tunes supervise only the first 16 of the 40 generated steps (chunk_size 16), so the 24-step overlap that ACC, STAC and RTC look at is untouched by our adapters code.

1. Foundations, with derivations

1.1 Continuous normalising flows: an ODE moves a density

A time-dependent vector field v(x, t) on ℝd defines a flow map ψt by the ODE

d⁄dt ψt(x) = v(ψt(x), t),   ψ0(x) = x(1)

Push a starting density p0 (here 𝒩(0, I)) through it: pt = [ψt]# p0. Probability mass is conserved and moves with velocity v, so pt satisfies the continuity equation:

∂t pt(x) + ∇·( pt(x) v(x, t) ) = 0(2)

Why: for any fixed region Ω, the rate of change of its mass equals minus the flux through its boundary, d⁄dt∫Ωpt = −∮∂Ω ptv·n. The divergence theorem then gives (2) pointwise. Conversely, a field v "generates" a path pt if and only if the pair satisfies (2) (under regularity conditions). This "iff" is what flow matching leans on.

Along one trajectory xt = ψt(x0), expanding (2) as ∂tp + v·∇p + p ∇·v = 0 gives the instantaneous change of variables (Chen et al. 2018):

d⁄dt log pt(xt) = −∇·v(xt, t)   ⟹   log p1(x1) = log p0(x0) − ∫01 ∇·v(xt, t) dt(3)

Equation (3) means a flow policy has an exact action likelihood log p(a | o). Integrate the ODE backward from a to ε and accumulate the divergence, estimating the trace with Hutchinson's ε⊤Jε. FAIL-Detect's logpO is a one-step, first-order version of this on observation embeddings (docs/FAIL_DETECT_MATHS.html §3.4).

The old way to train a CNF was maximum likelihood through (3), which needs an ODE solve per gradient step. Flow matching removes the solver from training.

1.2 Flow matching and conditional flow matching

Pick a target probability path pt from noise p0 to data q = p1, and a field ut that generates it. The ideal objective is plain regression:

ℒFM(θ) = 𝔼t, x~pt ‖ vθ(x, t) − ut(x) ‖²(4)

We know neither pt nor ut in closed form. Lipman et al.'s construction builds them from per-sample paths. For each data point x1, choose a simple conditional path pt(x|x1) with a known generating field ut(x|x1), and define the marginals

pt(x) = ∫ pt(x|x1) q(x1) dx1,    ut(x) = ∫ ut(x|x1) pt(x|x1) q(x1)⁄pt(x) dx1 = 𝔼[ ut(x|x1) | xt = x ](5)

Claim 1: the marginal field generates the marginal path. The continuity equation is linear in (p, pv), so

∂tpt(x) = ∫ ∂tpt(x|x1) q dx1 = −∫ ∇·( pt(x|x1) ut(x|x1) ) q dx1 = −∇·( pt(x) ut(x) )(6)

The last equality is the definition (5) of ut. So if each conditional path ends at δ(x − x1), the marginal ODE carries p0 to q.

Claim 2: you can regress on the conditional field instead. The conditional objective is

ℒCFM(θ) = 𝔼t, x1~q, x~pt(·|x1) ‖ vθ(x, t) − ut(x|x1) ‖²(7)

Expand both squares. The ‖vθ‖² terms are identical expectations, because (x1, x) ~ q·pt(·|x1) has x-marginal pt. The cross terms are equal by (5):

𝔼pt(x)⟨vθ(x,t), ut(x)⟩ = ∫⟨vθ, ∫ut(x|x1)pt(x|x1)q(x1)dx1⟩dx = 𝔼q, pt(·|x1)⟨vθ(x,t), ut(x|x1)⟩(8)

What is left differs only by terms that do not depend on θ:

ℒCFM(θ) = ℒFM(θ) + 𝔼t,x tr Cov[ ut(x|x1) | xt = x ]   ⟹   ∇θℒCFM = ∇θℒFM(9)

Two consequences matter later:

  • The minimiser is a conditional mean. v*(x, t) = 𝔼[ut(x|x1) | xt = x]. Flow matching averages the per-sample targets at each (x, t), but it does not average the samples. Different starting noises still land in different modes. This is why flow and diffusion heads avoid the "mode averaging" of a plain MSE regressor, a point VLA-FAIL's introduction makes in so many words paper.
  • The loss has a floor equal to the Cov term in (9). This is the irreducible part. A training loss can therefore stop falling because the data are inconsistent, not because the model is weak (§3.1).

1.3 Gaussian conditional paths, and the one every VLA uses

Take pt(x|x1) = 𝒩(μt(x1), σt²I). The flow x = μt + σtε generates it, and differentiating gives the conditional field

ut(x|x1) = σ′t⁄σt (x − μt(x1)) + μ′t(x1)(10)

Lipman's "OT path" is μt = t x1, σt = 1 − (1 − σmin)t. It gives ut(x|x1) = (x1 − (1 − σmin)x) ⁄ (1 − (1 − σmin)t). It is called OT because, for one x1, it is the optimal-transport displacement between two Gaussians: particles move in straight lines at constant speed. With σmin → 0 and the conditioning written in ε, this is the path every VLA here uses:

xt = (1 − t) ε + t a,    ut = a − ε = (a − xt)⁄(1 − t),    ℒ = 𝔼t, ε, (o,a) ‖ vθ(xt, t, o) − (a − ε) ‖²(11)

Two rewritings of (11) are used repeatedly below derived. Write the model's implied predictions of data and noise as

âθ(x, t) = x + (1 − t) vθ(x, t),    ε̂θ(x, t) = x − t vθ(x, t)(12)
‖vθ − (a − ε)‖² = ‖âθ − a‖²⁄(1 − t)² = ‖ε̂θ − ε‖²⁄t²(13)

So the velocity loss is a data-prediction loss weighted by 1/(1 − t)², or equivalently a noise-prediction loss weighted by 1/t². "Predict v, x or ε" is a choice of weighting over time, not of model class (§1.6).

For a conditional distribution p(a|o), the floor in (9) becomes

𝔼 tr Cov[a − ε | xt, o] = 𝔼 tr Cov[a | xt, o]⁄(1 − t)²,    which equals tr Cov[a | o] at t = 0(14)

derived If each observation has exactly one demonstrated chunk (a delta conditional), the floor is 0 and a perfect model has zero loss. If two demonstrations disagree at the same o, the loss at small t cannot go below their spread.

1.4 Rectified flow: straight paths and reflow

Liu, Gong & Liu reach the same objective from a different angle. Take any coupling (X0, X1) and the linear interpolation Xt = t X1 + (1 − t)X0, and fit

minv ∫01 𝔼 ‖ (X1 − X0) − v(Xt, t) ‖² dt   ⟹   v*(x, t) = 𝔼[X1 − X0 | Xt = x](15)

With the independent coupling X0 ~ 𝒩, this is exactly (11). The individual lines are straight, but the marginal ODE paths are not. Where lines from different pairs cross, v* averages their directions and the trajectory bends. Two results from the paper paper:

  • The rectified coupling (Z0, Z1) = (X0, ODEv*(X0)) has the same marginals and no greater convex transport cost.
  • Reflow retrains on that coupling, which makes the paths straighter. Straightness is measured by
S(Z) = ∫01 𝔼 ‖ (Z1 − Z0) − d⁄dtZt ‖² dt,    S = 0 ⟺ one Euler step is exact(16)

For a VLA derived: conditioned on o, if p(a|o) is a single tight mode, the conditional lines all point at nearly the same a, so the marginal path is nearly straight. That is why 4 steps (GR00T) or 10 (π0) suffice. Curvature, and therefore few-step error, appears where p(a|o) is multimodal, at the time when trajectories "decide" between modes.

1.5 Stochastic interpolants

Albergo & Vanden-Eijnden generalise the interpolation:

xt = αt x0 + βt x1 + γt z,   α0 = β1 = 1, α1 = β0 = γ0 = γ1 = 0,   z ~ 𝒩(0, I)(17)
b(x, t) = 𝔼[ α̇tx0 + β̇tx1 + γ̇tz | xt = x ],    s(x, t) = ∇log pt(x) = −γt−1 𝔼[z | xt = x](18)

Both b and s are learned by quadratic regression, exactly as in (15). The marginal density then obeys the transport equation for b, and for any diffusion coefficient ϵt ≥ 0 the family

dXt = [ b(Xt, t) + ϵt s(Xt, t) ] dt + √(2ϵt) dWt(19)

has the same marginals paper. The deterministic flow (ϵ = 0) and stochastic samplers are interchangeable at the level of marginals; they differ in how errors propagate. Flow matching (11) is the special case αt = 1 − t, βt = t, γ = 0 with Gaussian x0. In that case the score is free from the velocity derived:

s(x, t) = −𝔼[ε | xt = x]⁄(1 − t) = −(x − t v(x, t))⁄(1 − t)(20)

This uses ε̂ from (12). Any trained flow head can therefore also be run as an SDE sampler, or scored with score-based tools, without retraining inferred.

1.6 Relation to diffusion and score matching

Song et al.'s SDE view writes a forward noising process dx = f(x, t)dt + g(t)dW. Its marginals are also produced by the deterministic probability-flow ODE paper:

dx⁄dt = f(x, t) − ½ g(t)² ∇x log pt(x)(21)

A Gaussian diffusion has xt = αta + σtε. Its PF-ODE velocity is α̇t 𝔼[a|x] + σ̇t 𝔼[ε|x], which is (18) with γ = 0. Flow matching with the linear path is a diffusion model with αt = t, σt = 1 − t, trained with a particular weighting and sampled with its PF-ODE. The prediction targets are affine reparametrisations of one another. Write ŝ for the score, x̂ for the data prediction and ε̂ for the noise prediction:

ε̂ = −σt ŝ,   x̂ = (x − σtε̂)/αt,   v = α̇tx̂ + σ̇tε̂  (FM: v = x̂ − ε̂)(22)

Two meanings of "v-prediction". Salimans & Ho define v = αtε − σtx for variance-preserving schedules (α² + σ² = 1). That is the velocity along a quarter circle, not the rectified-flow velocity a − ε. They are the same idea (the time derivative of the path) on different paths. Do not port a "v-pred" weighting between the two without converting.

Loss weighting. Kingma & Gao show that the common diffusion objectives are all

ℒw = ½ 𝔼ε, λ [ w(λ) · (−dλ⁄dt) · ‖ε̂θ(xλ) − ε‖² ],   λ = log(αt²/σt²)(23)

The objectives differ only in the weighting w(λ) over log-SNR, and with monotone w they are ELBOs on augmented data paper. For the FM path, λ = 2 log(t/(1 − t)) derived. The FM velocity loss under a time density p(t) is, by (13),

ℒFM = 𝔼t~p(t) 1⁄t² ‖ε̂θ − ε‖² = 𝔼t~p(t) 1⁄(1 − t)² ‖âθ − a‖²(24)

So the effective weight on data-prediction error at time t is p(t)/(1 − t)². The time sampler and the parametrisation together set it, which matters for FACT (§3.4).

1.8 Sampling: Euler, Heun, and the few-step error

The sampler integrates dx/dt = vθ(x, t) on a grid 0 = t0 < … < tK = 1, step hk = tk+1 − tk:

Euler:  xk+1 = xk + hk vθ(xk, tk)     Heun:  x̃ = xk + hkvk,   xk+1 = xk + ½hk[vk + vθ(x̃, tk+1)](25)

Euler has global error O(h) at 1 network call per step; Heun has O(h²) at 2 calls per step (Karras et al. recommend Heun for diffusion ODEs). For a field with Lipschitz constant L in x, the standard bound on the Euler endpoint error is

‖xK − x(1)‖ ≤ h⁄2 · sup ‖d²x⁄dt²‖ · (eL − 1)⁄L(26)

Here d²x/dt² is the path curvature; it is zero for straight paths (16). Benton et al. bound the Wasserstein-2 distance between the generated and true distributions by the L2 velocity error times an exponential in the Lipschitz constant paper (title and abstract only). Velocity error, not just loss, controls sample error, and it can be amplified.

Two exact identities for the linear path derived. Both are central to §3.1.

(i) Last-step identity:   xK = xK−1 + (1 − tK−1) vθ(xK−1, tK−1) = âθ(xK−1, tK−1)(27)

The Euler output is the model's one-shot data prediction (12) at the last grid point, evaluated at the state the sampler itself produced. This holds for any network, with no assumptions.

(ii) Straight-path exactness:   if v = (a − x)/(1 − t), then xk+1 − a = (xk − a) · (1 − tk+1)⁄(1 − tk) ⇒ xK = a for any K(28)

Now let vθ = v* + ek at step k, for a delta conditional. Then δk+1 = δk(1 − tk+1)/(1 − tk) + hkek, and the product telescopes to zero at tK = 1:

xK − a = hK−1 eK−1   (for GR00T: 0.25 · e(x0.75, 0.75))(29)

The ideal field contracts toward a at rate 1/(1 − t), which erases earlier velocity errors. Only the last one survives. With a learned field, earlier errors matter only through which state xK−1 the sampler reaches, and, if p(a|o) is multimodal, through which mode basin it has entered.

1.9 OT couplings: straightening by pairing

Tong et al. (OT-CFM) and Pooladian et al. (multisample FM) replace the independent coupling in (15) with a minibatch optimal-transport coupling. Each data point is paired with a nearby noise sample within the batch, which reduces crossing, makes marginal paths straighter and cuts the number of steps needed paper. For VLAs this buys little inferred. Conditioning on o already makes each conditional target nearly a point (§1.4), and VLA heads train on one (o, a) pair at a time, so minibatch couplings across different o would be meaningless. None of π0, π0.5 or GR00T uses OT coupling code.

1.10 One-step models: consistency, shortcut, MeanFlow

All three learn the integral of the flow rather than its derivative, so sampling needs one or two network calls.

  • Consistency models (Song et al. 2023; continuous-time version Lu & Song 2024) learn fθ(xt, t) ≈ the ODE endpoint, with the boundary condition f(x, tdata) = x. Training enforces self-consistency along a trajectory:
    ℒCM = 𝔼 d( fθ(xt+Δ, t+Δ), sg[fθ(x̂t, t)] ),   x̂t one ODE step from xt+Δ(30)
    Here sg is stop-gradient. Training is by distillation from a teacher field or standalone. FAIL-Detect's CFM scorer adds a penalty of this kind to a flow.
  • Shortcut models (Frans et al. 2024) condition the network on a step size d as well and enforce that one step of size 2d equals two steps of size d:
    sθ(xt, t, 2d) = ½ sθ(xt, t, d) + ½ sθ(xt + d·sθ(xt, t, d), t + d, d)(31)
    At d → 0 the network is trained with ordinary flow matching. One model can then sample with 1, 2, 4, … steps.
  • MeanFlow (Geng et al. 2025) learns the average velocity over an interval and derives a training target from the identity obtained by differentiating its definition:
    u(z, r, t) = 1⁄(t − r)∫rt v(zτ, τ)dτ  ⟹  u = v(zt, t) − (t − r) d⁄dtu(zt, r, t)(32)
    One step is then zr = zt − (t − r)u(zt, r, t). The d/dt term is a Jacobian-vector product. Their sign and time conventions follow the paper's own (noise at 1).

Relevance to VLAs inferred. GR00T already runs at K = 4, and its output is the 0.75-step data prediction (27), so it is effectively a near-one-step model with three refinement steps. A one-step head matters most for detectors that need many samples: STAC uses 256 chunks per step (§3.3). It also matters for RTC-style guidance, which backpropagates through the denoiser at every step.

2. Flow matching as a VLA action head

2.1 π0 and π0.5

Training code pi0/modeling_pi0.py:593–641, common/flow_matching.py. This uses openpi time (noise at 1):

time  = Beta(1.5, 1.0).sample() * 0.999 + 0.001           # sample_time_beta(scale=0.999, offset=0.001)
x_t   = time * noise + (1 - time) * actions               # :595
u_t   = noise - actions                                    # :596
v_t   = action_out_proj(expert(prefix=[img, lang], suffix=[state, x_t ⊕ time]))
loss  = mse(u_t, v_t, reduction="none")                    # :641, reduced by the caller

Beta(1.5, 1) has density ∝ τ1/2, so in this convention mass sits near τ = 1, which is noise. That is the same emphasis as the paper's Beta((s − τ)/s; 1.5, 1) with τ = 0 as noise derived.

Inference code euler_integrate: dt = −1/num_steps, time = 1 + step·dt, x_t += dt·v_t, 10 steps by default (configuration_pi0.py:44, configuration_pi05.py:59). The prefix KV cache is computed once and only the action suffix is recomputed per step paper code denoise_step.

Architecture paper:

  • A ~300M-parameter action expert (a second set of transformer weights) shares attention layers with the 3B PaliGemma VLM.
  • Attention is blockwise causal:
    • [images + language] attend among themselves;
    • [state] attends to those;
    • [noisy actions] attend to everything, bidirectionally within the chunk.
    The VLM never attends to action tokens (att_masks += [1] + [0]*(chunk_size−1), modeling_pi0.py:583 code).
  • H = 50. Execution is 16 or 25 actions per inference depending on control rate.

π0 vs π0.5 in the code code:

  • π0 concatenates the sinusoidal time embedding with the action embedding and passes the result through an MLP (action_time_mlp_in/out, :529–590).
  • π0.5 instead passes the time embedding through time_mlp_in/out and injects it as adaRMS conditioning into every expert layer's norms (adarms_cond = time_emb, pi05/modeling_pi05.py:693–694).
  • π0.5's suffix embedding takes no continuous state. That is consistent with the paper's discretised state in the language prefix inferred.

The knowledge-insulation recipe (Driess et al.) trains the VLM on discrete FAST action tokens and stops the expert's flow gradient from flowing into the VLM paper (abstract only).

Native RTC in GR00T code :665–681, modeling_groot.py:347–442. If the previous chunk's leftovers are passed in:

  • the first overlap rows of the initial noise are overwritten with those clean actions;

2.3 Diffusion Policy, for contrast

code diffusion/modeling_diffusion.py:360–386, defaults in configuration_diffusion.py:139–150:

  • DDPM with 100 discrete training steps and a squared-cosine β schedule;
  • ε-prediction: the loss is MSE(ε̂, ε) at a uniformly sampled integer timestep;
  • clipped samples;
  • at inference, noise_scheduler.step over num_inference_steps, which defaults to all 100. DDIM can be swapped in for fewer steps;

By (22)–(24) this is the same family as flow matching, with a different path (variance-preserving), parametrisation (ε) and time weighting (uniform over discrete steps), and a stochastic sampler. The practical differences are many more sampling steps and no straight-path argument for few-step sampling.

3.1 (a) D20: the loss fell ~65%, but the sampled first chunk moved ~10%

The data ours (runs/r056_diag/loss_summary.json; docs/R056_POSTMORTEM.html). The loss is mean flow-matching loss at random t (the training Beta), with a fixed seed: 8 (t, ε) draws per frame and n = 100 frames per subset, model in eval mode (experiments/r056_diag_loss.py).

The sampled first chunk on held-out seed-0 starts moved toward the nominal chunk in 30 of 36 starts, but the median distance went only 4.51 → 3.98 (r16) and 4.12 (r4). That is 9–12% of the way ours post-mortem.

When can a lower flow loss at random t fail to produce a better sampled chunk? There are five mechanisms, which can be told apart. The first two are not about flow matching at all.

M1. Squared versus linear derived. The loss is a mean squared velocity error, while chunk distance is linear in the error. By (13), at fixed t, the data-prediction error is (1 − t)·(velocity error). If the error shrank uniformly over t, the endpoint error would shrink by √(loss ratio):

√(0.055/0.159) = 0.59,   √(0.059/0.159) = 0.61,   √(0.081/0.159) = 0.72  ⟹  expected movement on training starts ≈ 28–41%, not 65%(34)

The means are also tail-dominated. On IPEC the mean fell 55–62% while the median rose slightly, so the "65%" is mostly a few large-error frames being fixed. This alone does not explain 10%, but it shrinks the gap by about half.

M2. Training starts versus held-out starts (the confound). The loss is measured on the frames the adapter was trained on. The 10% is measured on starts it never saw. A model can fit its 100 training starts (D20(i): frame 0 fitted, 91–96% of frames below base) and still generalise weakly to new offsets. That is the coverage hypothesis (D20(ii): 42% of held-out starts lie beyond 12 cm, where training demos are scarce). No property of flow matching is needed to explain this.

The fit test has now run and confirms M2 ours (runs/r056_diag/fit_firstchunk.json, read 2026-10-07). The metric is the chunk-0 commanded translation ‖Σh<16 ah,1:3 − ref‖ in env action units, with medians over starts. Its anchor reproduces 4.514 / 3.979 / 4.120.

On its own training starts, the sampled chunk moved about as far as M1's √(loss ratio) predicts (30% against a predicted 28–41%). On held-out directions it barely moved. The loss-to-sample link is therefore intact, and the D20 tension is mostly generalisation across directions and offsets, which is a data-coverage and dose problem, not a flow-matching one. Two caveats:

  • the far training bands hold only 7 and 2 starts;
  • the label equals the nominal chunk at seed 0 but differs at seeds 1 and 2 (label_vs_nominal_seed0).

M3. The time slice that sets the sample derived. By (27), the executed chunk is âθ(x0.75, 0.75). The loss (33) averages over all t with the Beta weights of §1.7. Two facts pull in opposite directions:

  • By draw count, only 12.4% of draws land in [0.75, 1] and 35% in [0, 0.25). Most of a 65% drop could come from the noise end. There the optimal velocity is v*(ε, 0) = 𝔼[a|o] − ε, so learning a mean shift in 𝔼[a|o] is enough to cut the loss.
  • By weight on endpoint error, (24) gives an effective weight p(t)/(1 − t)² ∝ (1 − t)−3/2, which grows toward the data end. Per unit of endpoint error, a draw at t = 0.75 counts 8× more than one at t = 0.

Without per-t losses we cannot tell which end drove the drop. Cheap test: recompute the D20 loss with t fixed at the four sampler times {0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75}, same frames and same ε. This is one extra flag on r056_diag_loss.py inferred, and it reports the endpoint error (1 − t)·√loss at each.

There is also exposure bias. The loss evaluates âθ at the training interpolant x = 0.25ε + 0.75a, but the sampler evaluates it at x0.75 produced by its own first three steps. On held-out starts, those steps are driven by a field that has not learned the new label, so x0.75 sits in a region the training interpolant never visited.

M4. Mode mixing inferred, a hypothesis. By (9), v* is a conditional mean over targets that share (xt, o). Suppose two kinds of training frame have nearly the same observation but different labels. Here those are minted frames (nominal-run chunk as label) and 1:1 replay frames from IPEC, or the base model's own learned behaviour. The flow then represents a mixture. The loss on minted frames can fall because the model now puts mass on the minted mode, while a fresh ε lands in that mode only with the mixture weight πminted. The expected movement of the median sampled chunk is then roughly πminted times the full distance, and with frame 0 at ~1% of draws (post-mortem §3.1) π could be small.

  • Signature: a bimodal spread of sampled first chunks across ε at a fixed start, some near nominal and some near base, rather than a uniform 10% shift.
  • Test: sample 8–16 ε per start at fixed observation (frozen-obs path, as in V3) and look at the distribution of distances.
  • Free proxy: a K = 1 sample returns âθ(ε, 0) ≈ 𝔼[a|o], the mean of the mixture. A large K = 1 vs K = 4 difference flags multimodality.

M5. Conditioning shift between loss and rollout inferred. Several things differ between the loss frames and live rollouts:

  • The loss frames' observations are minted-rollout renders through the training preprocessor (augmentation off, checked in loss_summary.json). Live rollouts render fresh, with the R-039 render jitter.
  • Training applied state dropout (if not overridden), which inference never does.
  • Time is bucketed in both paths, so that is not a source.

The wrist camera carries the start-pose signal (W = 0.88, R-042), so small pixel differences at the wrist are exactly what the adapter needs to read. This is a minor candidate unless the fit test shows training-start renders and live renders diverge.

Reading order, after the fit test. M1 (arithmetic) and M2 (coverage, now confirmed) together account for the tension: the adapter fits training starts about as far as its loss implies and does not transfer to new directions. M3 and M4 remain cheap secondary checks for the next fine-tune: the per-t loss at sampler times, and multi-ε sampling at frozen observations. Neither needs training. M5 is last.

3.2 (b) Noise as a source of rollout variance, and why splices fix it

For fixed weights and observation, the sampler is a deterministic map of the initial noise:

A = Φo(ε),   ε ~ 𝒩(0, I40×132),   Φo = 4 Euler steps of vθ(·, ·, o)(35)

GR00T draws ε from an unseeded torch.randn at every forward (groot_n1_7.py:657) code. How much ε matters depends on p(a|o) derived:

  • for a tight unimodal conditional, Φo is nearly constant in ε (by (28), every ε lands near the same a);
  • near a mode boundary (grasp now or reposition, approach from the left or the right), ε chooses the basin, and a closed-loop rollout amplifies that choice.

Outcome flips under resampling are therefore partly a multimodality signal, not just noise. Render jitter is a second, independent source: R-039's two passes shared the seed and the fixed noise and still agreed on outcome in only 23/30 rollouts, while the action-level measurements agreed to within 0.06 ours. The working figure in RESULTS.md is a ~30% outcome flip rate at one seed. R-048 measured 43% without correction for the camera row.

Why the splices must share ε derived. R-039's transfer fraction is τ = 1 − ‖aX − aN‖ / ‖aP − aN‖ (vla_harness/capture/splice.py). If each arm drew its own noise:

aP − aN = [ΦoP(ε) − ΦoN(ε)] + [ΦoN(ε) − ΦoN(ε′)](36)

The second bracket is pure sampler spread. It enters both the numerator and the denominator of τ, biasing τ toward 0 and adding variance that has nothing to do with the spliced input. Fixing ε, by seeding each arm with torch.manual_seed(noise_key(forward)) and restoring the generator afterwards, deletes that bracket exactly. The model-level determinism gate (bitwise-identical action_pred for identical inputs and seed) makes this auditable ours RESULTS R-039. The same logic explains why single-draw STAC on R-041 was noise-dominated. Its distance between consecutive chunks contains a bracket like the second one at every step (§3.3).

Two different noise mechanisms exist in the harness, and they must not be mixed:

  • capture (groot_features.py) leaves the model's own draw untouched and replays it (save, draw, rewind), so rollouts are unchanged;
  • splice seeds the draw.

3.4 (d) FACT: what the "flow-matching policy training mismatch" is

paper (arXiv:2608.01402; HTML read for the relevant sections).

  • The claim. Sub-millimetre contact corrections are generated in the low-noise part of the flow. The Beta(1.5, 1) schedule of π0, π0.5 and SmolVLA allocates "only 8.9% of gradient signal to τ < 0.2" (τ = noise level, openpi convention). The model is therefore under-trained exactly where precision is decided, and actions "collapse to near-zero magnitude" in contact.
  • The fix. At post-training, swap the schedule for a logit-normal τ = σ(s z − m) with m = 1.5 and s = 1. They report 6× more mass at τ < 0.2, with no architecture change and no new data. A force pathway via adaRMS is added separately.
  • Results (π0.5, five contact-rich tasks): 39% → 66% average.
  • How they localised it. Force profiles and delta-collapse plots. The paper does not measure gradient flow per noise level.

Recomputed derived:

  • Under Beta(1.5, 1), P(τ < 0.2) = 0.21.5 ≈ 0.089, matching their 8.9%.
  • Under their LN, P(τ < 0.2) = Φ(logit 0.2 + 1.5) = Φ(0.114) ≈ 0.545, a 6.1× ratio, matching their "6×". An unshifted LN would give 0.083.
  • So "8.9% of gradient signal" means 8.9% of draws.

What it means for GR00T derived inferred:

  • Same schedule, same number. GR00T uses the identical Beta. In its convention the near-data fifth is t > 0.8, which also receives 8.9% of draws (§1.7).
  • Two reasons it may matter less than for π0.5.
    1. GR00T's 4-step sampler never queries t > 0.75. The output is the data prediction at t = 0.75 (27), so draws above 0.8 shape the sample only through weights shared across t. π0.5 at 10 steps queries down to openpi τ = 0.1.
    2. Per unit of endpoint error, the velocity loss already up-weights late t by 1/(1 − t)² (24). "Fewer draws" is not "less gradient" in endpoint units.
  • A shift toward the data end could still help where precision fails. Scene 1327's grasp closes 5.5 cm high, against 4.2 cm for base (post-mortem). But that failure is traced to polluted minted labels, not a schedule. If tried, it must be a pre-registered arm. Changing p(t) for a LoRA on ~100 demos also changes what the adapter does at the noise end, where π0's rationale says mode selection happens.

3.5 (e) Real-time chunking and inpainting for chunked flow policies

The problem paper (Black, Galliker & Levine). Inference takes d control steps. The next chunk must therefore agree with the d actions already committed, and it should not switch strategy across the rest of the overlap. Naive asynchronous execution switches mode at chunk boundaries. Temporal ensembling averages modes and produces invalid actions.

RTC as inpainting with guidance. Treat the previous chunk's overlap Y as a partial observation and correct the velocity with a pseudo-inverse guidance (ΠGDM) term:

v̂(Aτ) = v(Aτ) + min(β, (1 − τ)⁄(τ rτ²)) · (Y − Â1)⊤ diag(W) · ∂Â1⁄∂Aτ,   rτ² = (1 − τ)²⁄(τ² + (1 − τ)²)(37)

Here Â1 = Aτ + (1 − τ)v is exactly the data prediction (12). The weight W is a soft mask:

  • 1 on the first d steps (frozen);
  • exponentially decaying across the rest of the overlap;
  • 0 after H − s.

The guidance weight β is clipped (≈ 5 with few steps), and 5 denoising steps are used for π0.5. The paper finds hard masking of only the first d steps insufficient: a small d gives weak guidance, so the chunk can still switch strategy.

In code code:

  • LeRobot's generic RTC (rtc/modeling_rtc.py:205–243) implements (37) for π0, π0.5 and SmolVLA. It works in openpi time with tau = 1 − time, the vector-Jacobian product by autograd, LINEAR or EXP prefix schedules, and max_guidance_weight 10.
  • GR00T's native RTC (§2.2) does something simpler. It writes the clean prefix into the initial noise and damps the velocity there (0 when frozen, an exponential ramp elsewhere), with no guidance gradient. Feeding clean actions as if they were noise at t = 0 is off the training distribution for those rows inferred.

Relevance to us inferred:

  • Delay. Simulation is synchronous (d = 0), and our harness passes no leftovers, so every chunk is sampled from fresh ε with no continuity constraint. Chunk-boundary mode switches are therefore possible every 16 steps. That is the effect ACC measures and RTC suppresses.
  • Start pose. R-057 found the episode decided at forward 0 (k* = 1), and there is no previous chunk at forward 0. RTC cannot fix the start-pose failure. It is relevant only to mid-episode thrash, such as scene 1327's regrasp cycles.
  • Overlap supervision. Inpainting needs the model to be good on the overlapped tail (steps 17–40), which our adapters never trained (§2.2).

Glossary

termmeaning here
Velocity field v(x, t)The network's output. Integrating dx/dt = v from noise produces an action chunk.
Probability path ptThe distribution of xt at time t, from 𝒩(0, I) to the data.
Continuity equation∂tp + ∇·(pv) = 0, the statement that mass is conserved while being moved by v. A field generates a path iff they satisfy it.
CFMConditional flow matching: regress on per-sample targets; same gradient as the intractable marginal loss (9).
Rectified flow / reflowLinear interpolation between coupled pairs; reflow retrains on the model's own coupling to straighten paths.
Stochastic interpolantxt = αx0 + βx1 + γz; gives both ODE and SDE samplers with the same marginals.
PF-ODEProbability-flow ODE: the deterministic sampler that shares a diffusion's marginals.
ε-, x-, v-predictionAffine reparametrisations of one predictor; they differ in the implied time weighting (13), (22).
âθ(x, t)Implied one-shot data prediction x + (1 − t)vθ. GR00T's executed chunk is this at t = 0.75 (27).
Time schedule p(t)How training draws t. GR00T and π0 use a Beta(1.5, 1) emphasising the noise end; FACT shifts toward the data end.
NFE / KNetwork evaluations / denoising steps at inference. GR00T K = 4, π0 10, Diffusion Policy 100.
StraightnessS in (16); zero means one Euler step is exact.
Mode averagingWhat MSE regression does to multimodal targets. Flow matching averages velocities, not samples, so it avoids it.
Fixed-noise spliceRunning several input variants with the same ε so action differences reflect only the inputs (36).
RTCReal-time chunking: inpainting the next chunk to agree with the committed overlap, via guided velocity (37).
Exposure biasThe sampler evaluates the network at its own intermediate states, not at the training interpolants.
