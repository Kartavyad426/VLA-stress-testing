Fine-Tuning 101

2026-09-28 · A general primer on fine-tuning large pretrained models: what to update, how to decide, what it costs in memory, and how to keep what the model already knew. Part II applies it to the GR00T N1.7 start-pose retraining plan (RETRAINING_PLAN.html). Section 9 covers Soup and its layer-streaming trainer. Parameter counts for GR00T are read from the checkpoint's safetensors header; everything marked estimate is arithmetic, not a measurement.
Summary
  • Fine-tuning is choosing Δθ. You keep the pretrained weights θ₀ and learn a small change Δθ from new data. Every method differs in which coordinates of Δθ are allowed to be non-zero (a subset of layers, a low-rank matrix, a bias vector) and in how that change is regularised towards θ₀.
  • Decide by locating the gap, not by size alone. Ask three questions in order. Is the needed information already present in the frozen features? (Probe for it.) Where does the distribution shift enter: the input side or the output side? (Tune the block nearest to it.) What does the compute budget allow? Tune the fewest parameters that can express the fix.
  • The main risk is regression, not failure to learn. Small targeted datasets overwrite general behaviour. The standard defences are mixing in the original data (replay), low-rank or partial updates, a lower learning rate, and interpolating weights back towards θ₀ after training (WiSE-FT).
  • Memory is dominated by optimiser state, not weights. Adam with fp32 master weights costs about 16 bytes per trainable parameter; frozen parameters cost only their storage. That arithmetic is why LoRA, frozen backbones and 8-bit optimisers exist.
  • Soup is a fine-tuning CLI. Its headline feature, layer streaming, keeps a frozen base in CPU RAM and moves one decoder layer at a time onto the GPU, overlapping the copy with compute. It trained an 8B LLM with LoRA in 3.3 GB of VRAM. Each streamed layer is read twice per step, once forward and once backward, because gradients for lower layers must pass back through it.
  • For us: the map says the start-pose information survives the frozen VLM (R-045), so the plan freezes the VLM and trains only the action head. Three findings from reading the code: the head the port trains by default is 1.62 B parameters, not ~1.1 B (the plan's figure matches the diffusion transformer alone). GR00T's own LoRA fields are not wired. And arm (b)'s tune_top_llm_layers=4 fully unfreezes 201 M parameters rather than applying LoRA. Because the VLM is frozen in arm (a), its outputs can be cached once, which takes about 3 GB off the GPU and makes a local LoRA run comfortable (estimate).

1What fine-tuning is

A pretrained model is a point θ₀ in parameter space that already solves a broad task well: next-token prediction over the web, image-text alignment, or, for a VLA, action prediction over many robots. Fine-tuning continues gradient descent from θ₀ on a narrower dataset 𝒟ft, and stops long before the parameters travel far. The result is θ = θ₀ + Δθ.

θ* = θ₀ + argminΔθ ∈ 𝒮   𝔼(x,y)∼𝒟ft [ ℓ( fθ₀+Δθ(x), y ) ]  +  λ · R(Δθ)(1)

Every fine-tuning method is a choice of two things in (1):

  • The feasible set 𝒮. This is which changes are allowed. Full fine-tuning allows any Δθ. Freezing a layer forces its block of Δθ to zero. LoRA forces each weight update to have rank ≤ r. BitFit allows only bias entries to change.
  • The regulariser R. Often it is implicit: a small learning rate, few steps and early stopping all keep ‖Δθ‖ small. Sometimes it is explicit: weight decay towards θ₀ (L2-SP), a Fisher-weighted penalty (EWC), or a KL term to the original model's outputs.
Figure 1. Fine-tuning moves θ₀ a short distance into a region that also solves the new task. The good solutions (blue) stay close enough to θ₀ that the pretraining basin still holds. Travelling too far (orange) fits the new task but leaves the region where the old abilities lived. Schematic only.

Why it works at all: intrinsic dimension

Aghajanyan et al. (2020) measured how many parameters a fine-tune really needs. They restricted Δθ to a random d-dimensional subspace, Δθ = P·z with P fixed and random, and found the smallest d reaching 90% of full fine-tuning quality. For RoBERTa on GLUE tasks, d is in the hundreds to low thousands, against more than 10⁸ parameters. The better the pretraining, the smaller d gets. This is the empirical basis for every parameter-efficient method. A good pretrained model already contains the features, so the fine-tune only has to re-weight them.

For a VLA specifically

For a VLA the loss ℓ is behaviour cloning: predict the demonstrator's action chunk from observations. GR00T's action head is a flow-matching model. It learns a velocity field that carries Gaussian noise ε to the action chunk A, conditioned on the VLM features φ(o) and the robot state s:

Aτ = τ·A + (1−τ)·ε,    ℓFM = ‖ vθ(Aτ, τ, φ(o), s) − (A − ε) ‖²,    τ ∼ Beta-shaped schedule(2)

Two consequences matter later. First, the loss is a regression on continuous actions, so "correct" demonstrations must be consistent. Two demos that do different things from the same observation are averaged into a blur (or, with a flow model, into a bimodal distribution the policy samples from). Second, τ and ε are drawn fresh every step, but φ(o) does not depend on them. If the VLM that computes φ is frozen, φ(o) can be computed once and reused (§12).

3LoRA and its relatives, in depth

LoRA (Hu et al. 2021) parameterises the update to a weight matrix W₀ ∈ ℝd×k as a product of two thin matrices, and leaves W₀ frozen:

h = W₀x + (α/r)·B A x,    B ∈ ℝd×r, A ∈ ℝr×k, r ≪ min(d,k),    A ∼ 𝒩(0,σ²), B = 0 at init(3)
Figure 3. LoRA adds a trainable low-rank bypass beside a frozen matrix. Because B starts at zero, the model at step 0 is exactly the pretrained model. The example dimensions are those of GR00T's action-head DiT (hidden size 1536).

Design choices that matter

  • Which matrices. The original paper adapted only the attention Wq and Wv. Later practice (QLoRA and most VLA fine-tunes) adapts all linear layers, attention and MLP. That is usually worth the small extra cost: at a fixed parameter budget, more matrices at lower rank beat fewer matrices at higher rank.
  • Rank r and scale α. The update is scaled by α/r, so changing r at fixed α changes the effective learning rate. rsLoRA (Kalajdzievski 2023) shows that α/√r keeps the update magnitude stable as r grows, which makes high ranks actually useful. Common settings: r ∈ {8, 16, 32, 64}, α = r or 2r.
  • Learning rate. LoRA wants roughly 5–10× the learning rate of full fine-tuning (e.g. 1e-4 to 3e-4 against 1e-5 to 3e-5), because only a small, zero-initialised subspace moves. LoRA+ (Hayou et al. 2024) sets B's learning rate about 16× higher than A's, since the two matrices play asymmetric roles.
  • Dropout on the LoRA path (0.05–0.1) helps in the low-data regime.
  • Modules to train fully alongside LoRA (modules_to_save in PEFT). Small, task-specific modules such as a new output head, normalisation layers, or input and output projections for a new embodiment are often trained fully while the large matrices get LoRA. This is where memory budgets are quietly blown: a "small" projector with 300 M parameters trained fully with Adam costs about 5 GB.

What LoRA cannot do
A rank-r update to a frozen matrix can re-weight and recombine what the layer already computes. It cannot create features the frozen layers upstream never compute. If the information is lost before the adapted layers, no rank recovers it. Biderman et al. (2024), "LoRA Learns Less and Forgets Less", found that full fine-tuning learns weight perturbations of rank 10–100× higher than typical LoRA settings on hard targets (code, maths), and that LoRA underperforms there while better preserving the source domain. The question to ask is always whether the information is already present upstream (§4).

4How to decide what to fine-tune

The central decision is where the model is wrong, not how big it is. A fine-tune is a hypothesis about which part of the computation is missing. Train the smallest part that can express the fix, and use cheap diagnostics to find that part before training anything.

Figure 4. A decision procedure. Q1 is the cheapest and most informative step: a probe on frozen activations costs minutes and tells you whether a head-only fine-tune can work in principle.

Q1. Probing: is the information there?

Fit a small regressor or classifier from a block's activations to the variable the model is failing on. A good fit means the information exists at that depth, and the failure is in what comes after it. Kumar et al. (2022, LP-FT) add a caution: when features are good, full fine-tuning with a randomly initialised head can distort them, because the large early gradients from the bad head flow back into the backbone. Their fix is to train the head first with the backbone frozen (a linear probe), then unfreeze. That beats both methods alone out of distribution. This is a strong argument for "head first" whenever Q1 says yes.

A probe establishes that the information is decodable, not that the model uses it. The converse can also mislead: a linear probe can miss information that is present in non-linear form. Probe with a small MLP too, and compare against a control task (Hewitt & Liang 2019) to make sure the probe isn't doing the work itself.

Q2. Surgical fine-tuning: match the block to the shift

Lee et al. (2022) fine-tuned one contiguous block at a time under different distribution shifts. Tuning only the right block matched or beat full fine-tuning:

shift typeexamplebest block to tune
input-levelimage corruptions (CIFAR-C), new camera, sensor noisefirst block
feature-levelnew sub-populations of known classes (Entity-30)middle blocks
output-levelrelabelled or permuted outputs (CelebA spurious labels, CIFAR-Flip)last block / head

The intuition: a low-level shift is best absorbed by the layer that first sees it, before the error propagates. A change in the input-output rule is best absorbed at the end, where the rule is computed. Their heuristic for choosing automatically ranks layers by the relative gradient norm ‖∇θlℓ‖/‖θl‖ on the target data.

Q3. Budget

  • Data size. With 10²–10³ examples, prefer LoRA or partial fine-tuning; full fine-tuning overfits and forgets. With 10⁴–10⁵ and a large shift, full fine-tuning becomes worth it.
  • Memory (§6). The optimiser state for trainable parameters dominates.
  • Regression tolerance (§5). If the original behaviour must hold within a few points, restrict Δθ and mix in the original data.

Other orderings you'll meet

  • Gradual unfreezing (ULMFiT, Howard & Ruder 2018). Start with only the top layer trainable and unfreeze one more layer per epoch, top down. Combine with discriminative learning rates: lower layers get η·ξ−depth with ξ≈2.6. Both follow the same principle: lower layers are more general and should move less.
  • Layer-wise learning-rate decay (LLRD). The continuous version: ηl = η·γL−l, γ ∈ [0.65, 0.95]. It is standard for ViT and BEiT fine-tunes.

5Forgetting, and how to keep what the model knew

A fine-tune on a narrow slice pushes θ towards what that slice needs. Any direction the slice does not constrain is free to drift, and the general behaviour lives in those directions. This is catastrophic forgetting. In robotics it shows up as a nominal success rate that drops after you fix a perturbation.

Data-side defences
  • Replay / co-training. Mix a fraction of the original training data into every batch (10–50%). This is the strongest single defence, and the standard one for VLAs (OpenVLA, π₀ and GR00T all co-train).
  • Balanced sampling. Balance by task and by trajectory so a few long episodes don't dominate.
  • Hold out the nominal set and track it during training, not just at the end.
Parameter-side defences
  • Restrict Δθ: LoRA, partial unfreezing, low learning rate, few epochs.
  • Anchor to θ₀: L2-SP, i.e. λ‖θ−θ₀‖², or EWC's Fisher-weighted version λΣ Fi(θi−θ₀,i)².
  • Interpolate after training: WiSE-FT (below).
  • Average several fine-tunes: model soups (below).

WiSE-FT: weight-space interpolation

Wortsman et al. (2021) observed that the straight line between the pretrained and fine-tuned weights often has no loss barrier: every point on it is a working model. Evaluating along the line trades target gains against source retention, and the middle often beats both ends out of distribution:

θ(α) = (1−α)·θ₀ + α·θft,    α ∈ [0,1], chosen on held-out data(4)
Figure 5. Schematic of the WiSE-FT trade-off, not measured data. Moving from θ₀ to θft raises target success and lowers nominal success. An intermediate α often keeps most of the gain for a fraction of the regression.
Name clash: "model soups" are not "Soup"
Model soups (Wortsman et al. 2022) average the weights of several models fine-tuned from the same θ₀ with different hyperparameters. The "greedy soup" adds each model to the average only if held-out accuracy improves. The average is often better and more robust than the best single model, at no inference cost. A "learned soup" optimises one mixing coefficient per model per layer. That is the only "layer-by-layer soup" in the literature, and it is unrelated to the Soup CLI in §9, whose layer-by-layer feature is a memory technique, not a weight-averaging one.

6Memory arithmetic

Training memory has four terms. Only the first depends on frozen parameters:

M ≈ Pall·bw  +  Ptrain·(bmaster + bgrad + bopt)  +  activations(batch, seq, depth)  +  overhead(5)
regimeweightsfp32 mastergradAdam m, vper trainable param
fp32 everything4—4816 B
mixed precision (bf16 compute, fp32 master)242816 B
pure bf16 weights + fp32 Adam2—2812 B
bf16 + 8-bit Adam (bitsandbytes)2—226 B
frozen, bf162———2 B
frozen, NF4 (QLoRA)~0.55———~0.55 B

Rule of thumb: a trainable parameter costs 6–8× a frozen one. The levers, roughly in order of how much they save:

  1. Train fewer parameters (LoRA, freezing). This removes the 12–16 B/param term for everything frozen.
  2. Don't keep frozen parts on the GPU at all. If a frozen prefix of the network receives no gradient, precompute its outputs once and store them (feature caching). Or stream it in layer by layer (layer streaming, §9). Or keep it on the CPU (offloading, ZeRO-Offload).
  3. Quantise frozen weights (NF4, int8): about 4× on the frozen term.
  4. Gradient checkpointing. Store only block boundaries and recompute inside each block in the backward pass. Activation memory drops from O(L) to about O(√L) or O(1) per block, for about 30% more compute.
  5. 8-bit or paged optimisers: 8 B → 2 B for Adam state.
  6. Smaller micro-batch plus gradient accumulation: the same effective batch at constant activation memory.
  7. Shard across GPUs (ZeRO 1/2/3, FSDP). This doesn't apply to a single laptop GPU.

7Recipe: data, hyperparameters, schedule

Data
  • Consistency beats volume. Behaviour cloning averages contradictory demos. 100 consistent demos beat 500 noisy ones.
  • Match the observation pipeline exactly: resolution, crop, normalisation statistics, action convention (absolute or delta, frame), chunk length. A silent convention mismatch looks like "the fine-tune didn't work".
  • Cover the axis you want to fix, and a bit beyond, so held-out points are interpolation, not extrapolation.
  • Replay original data (§5).
Optimisation
  • Learning rate: full or partial 1e-5–1e-4; LoRA 1e-4–3e-4. Start from the pretraining recipe's value when known.
  • Warmup 3–10% of steps, then cosine or linear decay.
  • Weight decay small (1e-5–1e-2). Decay towards 0, not θ₀, unless using L2-SP.
  • Steps: measure in epochs over the new data. Overfitting starts within a handful of epochs at N≈500.
  • Gradient clipping at 1.0.
  • EMA of weights is often used for diffusion and flow heads; evaluate the EMA copy.
Training loss is not the metric
For policies, validation behaviour-cloning loss correlates weakly with closed-loop success. Small errors compound over a rollout, and flow and diffusion losses are noisy per sample. Save checkpoints at regular intervals and pick one by rollout success on a small held-out set, not by loss. Budget evaluation time accordingly: it usually exceeds training time for small fine-tunes.

8Evaluating a fine-tune

  1. Held-out by the axis that matters. Hold out whole instances (scene, start pose, object), not random frames of training episodes. Frame-level splits leak.
  2. Three measurements, always together: target slice (did it learn?), nominal control (did it forget?), and a neighbouring slice it was not trained on (did it generalise, or memorise?).
  3. Seeds and intervals. Report successes over n trials with a Wilson interval. At n=10, a 95% interval on 7/10 is 0.40–0.89, so differences under about 30 points need more trials or paired comparisons on the same instances and seeds.
  4. Continuous readouts alongside binary success (closest approach, time to grasp, action displacement at step 0). They carry information binary success throws away.
  5. Mechanism check. Test that the fine-tune fixed things the way you intended. Re-run the diagnostic that motivated it; the measure that located the gap should now look nominal.
  6. Ablate one step larger and one step smaller in trainable set and data size. A result that holds at only one configuration is fragile.

9Soup and layer streaming

Soup (soup-cli, Apache-2.0, v0.75 at the time of writing) is a command-line wrapper around Hugging Face transformers, peft and trl. One YAML file and soup train run SFT, DPO, GRPO, KTO and similar tasks with LoRA and quantisation. Most of it is packaging. The technically interesting part is layer streaming (training.stream_layers: true), a beta feature described in a preprint (Makazhan 2026, "Exact Layer Streaming: LoRA Fine-Tuning of an 8B Model on a 4 GB Laptop GPU", Zenodo). This is the "fine-tuning layer by layer": the model is still trained with ordinary LoRA, but the frozen base is moved to the GPU one decoder layer at a time.

The idea

Under LoRA, the base weights never change and never need optimiser state. They only need to be on the GPU while their layer is computing. A transformer computes one layer at a time. So peak VRAM for the base can be bounded by roughly two layers (the one computing and the one arriving) instead of all L.

Figure 6. Where things live under layer streaming. The frozen base sits in pinned host RAM. Two pre-allocated VRAM buffers double-buffer the layers. The trainable LoRA state, activations and logits stay resident as usual.

One training step

Soup's runtime describes a step as:

FORWARD   layer i: wait(i) -> prefetch(i+1) -> checkpoint(body_i)
BACKWARD  layer i: wait(i) -> prefetch(i-1) -> recompute + backward

While layer i computes on the compute stream, layer i+1 is copied host-to-device on a separate CUDA stream into the other buffer. Each layer body runs under gradient checkpointing, so only its input is saved. The backward pass walks the layers in reverse, re-streaming each one, recomputing its forward from the saved input, and back-propagating.

Figure 7. Double-buffered streaming for a four-layer toy. With pinned host memory, the copies overlap compute and the GPU stays busy (measured 97–100% utilisation). With pageable memory, the copies serialise (79%).

Why each layer is read twice

For a linear layer y = Wx, the gradient that must reach the layer below is ∂ℓ/∂x = Wᵀ·∂ℓ/∂y. So the backward pass needs W again for every layer that has any trainable parameter below it. Soup's source calls this "physics, not an implementation detail". Recomputing the forward also needs W. So a streamed step moves the whole base across PCIe twice. The corollary, which matters for us: layers below the lowest trainable parameter need no backward at all. They are read once, and if the input data is fixed, their output can be computed once for the whole dataset.

  • Speed cost: 1.43× slower than resident training at 0.5B, the only size where a resident baseline fits on their card. They report that an H100 replication at 8B ran at a similar 113 tok/s. Their v3 preprint retracted an earlier claim that streaming is bound by host-to-device transfer: removing every copy bought only 1.4%. The largest streaming-specific cost was NF4 dequantisation (9.8%).
  • Correctness: streamed forward passes are bit-exact against resident ones, and a 100-step loss curve matched. It is still worth noting what they found and fixed along the way: a silent wrong-gradient bug on large NF4 layers, and two releases whose saved adapters were empty or unloadable. It is a beta.
  • Pinned memory is the real bottleneck. Asynchronous copies need page-locked host memory. When the store did not fit under the pinnable limit, throughput fell by up to 6.6×. NF4 matters mostly because it makes the store small enough to pin.
  • Batch before accumulation. Every micro-batch re-reads the whole base, so raising the batch size amortises the reads. At the same effective batch of 4, batch=4 was 2.5× faster than batch=1 × accumulation 4.
  • Not for RL rollouts. GRPO and PPO are refused permanently, because generation re-reads every layer once per generated token.
Can we use Soup for GR00T?
Not directly. Soup streams text-only AutoModelForCausalLM decoders from a fixed list of architectures (Llama, Qwen2/3/3.5, Mistral, Gemma, Phi). GR00T N1.7 is a Qwen3-VL-style vision-language backbone plus a separate DiT action head with a flow-matching loss, trained through lerobot. Neither the model class nor the loss is supported. What transfers is the idea, and in our case a simpler relative of it applies: in arm (a) the entire VLM sits below the lowest trainable parameter, so it needs no backward pass and no streaming. It can be run once and cached (§12).

The two are different experiments; the plan should say which one it means.

Suggested additions to the recipe (proposals, not decided)

  • Replay. Mix the original 500 libero_spatial_no_noops demos into training (e.g. 1:1 by sampling weight). This is the cheapest guard on the "<5 pp nominal regression" prediction. It changes the data budget, so R-040's matched-N comparison must count it identically in every arm.
  • LoRA placement for arm (a). All linear layers of the DiT and vl_self_attention at r=16 (≈19 M params). Keep the projectors frozen at first: the embodiment and action convention do not change, and a 0.34 B projector trained fully costs about 5 GB of optimiser state. A second run with projectors trainable is the "one step larger" ablation.
  • WiSE-FT sweep after training: evaluate α ∈ {0.5, 0.75, 1.0} on the nominal control and on held-out starts. This costs no training, and it answers directly whether regression can be traded away.
  • Checkpoint selection by rollout on a small held-out validation set of starts, disjoint from the final held-out set, not by flow-matching loss.
  • Keep the mechanism check the plan already names: the wrist-arm transfer should fall on the retrained checkpoint.

Feature caching: the simplest win

In arm (a), nothing below the action head is trainable, so by §9's corollary the VLM needs a forward pass only, never a backward. The training loss (2) resamples τ and ε every step, but φ(o) depends only on the observation. So φ(o) for every training frame can be computed once, written to disk, and the VLM never loaded during training. That removes the 1.52 B-parameter backbone (about 3 GB in bf16) from the GPU and removes its forward from every step, so each step also runs faster.

Figure 11. Caching the frozen VLM's output. It is exact, not an approximation, provided the pipeline applies no random image augmentation (see caveats).
  • Size. Estimate: two 256×256 images at Qwen3-VL's 16-px patches with 2×2 merging give about 64 tokens each. Add about 20–40 text tokens, for ~170 tokens × 2048 × 2 bytes ≈ 0.7 MB per frame. At 500 episodes × ~150 frames that is about 50 GB of disk. Storing only the approach segment the plan targets cuts it several-fold. Verify the token count on one real forward before sizing anything.
  • Augmentation caveat. If GR00T's training transforms apply random crops or colour jitter to the images, a single cached φ(o) removes that augmentation. The options are to cache K augmented copies per frame, or to accept no augmentation for a 500-demo fine-tune. In the port's defaults, colour jitter and random rotation are off (groot_n1_7.py:94–98). image_crop_size is set, and whether that crop is random at train time is not yet verified.
  • Arm (b) caches at the input of layer 12 (the output of the frozen ViT plus LLM layers 0–11) and keeps layers 12–15 on the GPU with LoRA. The same logic applies, one boundary higher.
  • Relation to Soup. Streaming pays off when there are trainable parameters inside or below a large frozen stack, which forces a backward pass through it. Neither of our arms has that. So caching gives the whole benefit of streaming, and more, with no new dependency.
Consequence for the GPU-rental decision (PENDING #25)
The LoRA arms look local-feasible even without caching (≈7.4–8.0 GB, marginal), and comfortable with it (≈5–5.4 GB). Only a full head fine-tune needs a rented 24–40 GB card. That supports the coordinating session's suggestion: run the local memory smoke first, preferably of the cached-feature variant, and rent only if it fails or if the full-head arm is wanted as an ablation.
